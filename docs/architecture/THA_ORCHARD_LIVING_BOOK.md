# The Orchard Living Book — Canonical Design Reference

**Status:** CANONICAL DESIGN REFERENCE — the living account of what it feels like to live inside The Healthy Apples; recommended reading before any user-facing experience work, so that a designer, developer or contributor knows the *feeling* they are building toward
**Classification:** Experience Governance (canonical)
**Adopted:** 2026-07-15 (OLB1)
**Extends:** [`THA_EXPERIENCE_BLUEPRINT.md`](./THA_EXPERIENCE_BLUEPRINT.md) (the vision and the place) and [`THA_ORCHARD_HOUSE_DESIGN_BLUEPRINT.md`](./THA_ORCHARD_HOUSE_DESIGN_BLUEPRINT.md) (the design language) — this book is their **lived, felt account**; it adds no vision, no rule, no check, and owns no place, light, material, or orchard *law*, all of which those documents own
**Never overrides:** [`THA_EXPERIENCE_ARCHITECTURE.md`](./THA_EXPERIENCE_ARCHITECTURE.md) (behaviour) · [`THA_UI_ARCHITECTURE.md`](./THA_UI_ARCHITECTURE.md) (the binding look) · [`THA_EXPERIENCE_LANGUAGE.md`](./THA_EXPERIENCE_LANGUAGE.md) (the feeling) · the two blueprints it extends
**Enforced by:** nothing new. This book adds **no gate and no check.** The feeling it describes is already protected by the three governance gates, the Blueprint Checks (Experience Blueprint § 15.2), the Experience Test (§ 15.3) and the Design Character Check (Orchard House Design Blueprint § 16.2). This book is what those checks are *for* — the felt standard they exist to keep, written down so it cannot be forgotten.

> **What this book is.** The governing documents say what THA must *do*, *look like*, *feel like*, and *be* — as rules, so they can be checked. None of them says, in a voice a person can simply read and recognise, **what it is like to live in this house.** That is what this book holds. It is not architecture, not UI, and not a design system; it invents nothing and legislates nothing. It is the *living reference* — a set of chapters describing ordinary moments in the Orchard House, so that anyone building the next room already knows, before they read a single rule, the feeling they are trying to protect. It restates no rule from any governing document (restating a rule creates a second owner of it, which the architecture forbids — Experience Blueprint § 18; Architecture Principle 2). Where a chapter touches a governed concern — the one morning, the still orchard, the Companion's manners — it is *describing* what the rule already produces, never re-legislating it, and the rule's owner is cited once. Read it slowly. It is meant to be felt, not audited.

## How to read this book

The three governing documents divide the experience so it can be enforced: the Experience Architecture owns what it must *do*, the UI Architecture how it must *look*, the Experience Language how it must *feel*. The Experience Blueprint draws the house those three describe — *a modern home in an ancient orchard* — and the Orchard House Design Blueprint gives that house its design character. All five are, by design, written to be *checked*.

This book is written to be *lived*. It owns only one thing, which none of them owns: **the narrative, first-hand account of dwelling in the house across the moments of an ordinary life.** Everything it describes — the light, the materials, the orchard, the way the Companion behaves — is owned and specified elsewhere and cited, not restated. What is *new* here is only the telling: the felt experience of arriving, working, resting, and returning, written the way you would describe your own home to someone who has never been.

A note the book keeps to, because the whole house depends on it: **the house never changes.** One warm morning, one still orchard, one small palette of honest materials, forever (Experience Blueprint § 6, § 7; Orchard House Design Blueprint § 11). What *does* change is the household's own life — the day it is, the season they are in, the weather in *their* world, the mood they arrive carrying. So the chapters about rain, about the four seasons, about evening are not chapters about the house's weather or the orchard's calendar — the house has neither. They are chapters about *the household's* rain, *the household's* seasons, *the household's* evening, and about the one constant they return to through all of them. Time shows through the family's life, never through the house's light. That is not a poetic flourish; it is the law of the house, written here as feeling (Orchard House Design Blueprint § 11; Experience Blueprint § 6.0).

Each chapter moves, in prose, through the same lived questions the house always answers — *where you are, what is happening, how it feels, the quality of the light, your relationship to the orchard, the materials around you, and how the Companion keeps company* — and closes on the two things the book most wants a builder to carry: what must **never** happen in that moment, and the feeling the household should be left holding.

# The Chapters

## First Light

You come to the house at the very start of things, before the day has asked anything of you. You have not begun; you have *arrived*. The house was expecting you, and it says so gently, once, in a hand that looks written rather than typed — your name, the hour, nothing more. Nothing is demanded. There is no list waiting to pounce, no red number, no report standing to attention. There is only the sense of having stepped somewhere calm that already knew you were coming.

The light is the first thing you feel. It is morning — always morning here — a warm, low, upper-left light that falls the same way in every room and has fallen that way every time you have ever come. It is the light of a kitchen at the good hour, before the noise. Through the windows the orchard stands in it: mature, in leaf, unhurried, entirely at peace with being looked at and needing nothing from you. It was there before this visit and will be there after; you never have to tend it, and that is exactly why it steadies you.

Around you the surfaces are few and warm and plainly what they are — a clean plane in good light, generous with air, holding only what this moment needs. The Companion is nearby but has not spoken. It arrives a beat after you, the way a good friend lets you get your coat off before they say hello, and then waits at the counter, ready, saying nothing until there is something worth saying.

*Never here — a wall of metrics greeting you before you have arrived; a task thrust forward before the welcome; the greeting worn out by overuse until it means nothing.*
*What lingers — the feeling of being expected somewhere calm, and of the day not having started until you were ready to start it.*

## The View Into The Orchard

There is a moment, in the brightest room of the house, when you simply look out. The orchard is fully present here — not a picture on the wall but the world the house stands in, held open and generous, given room to be itself. You are not asked to do anything with it. You look, the way you would look up from the sink and out the window at something that has always been there and always will be.

The feeling is spaciousness, and a particular kind of trust: the trust that comes from a thing that does not change. The orchard is bright and growing and in season, and it is *still* — no drifting mist, no swaying branches, no performance to catch your eye. Its life is in its warmth and its light, never in movement. What you feel looking at it is not excitement but ease, the ease of a horizon that will keep.

The light out there is the same morning that lights the room behind you; there is one sun in this house and the orchard shares it. The materials near you stay quiet so the view can be generous — a small warm surface, plenty of air, nothing competing. This is the one room where the view is part of the point. Everywhere else in the house the orchard is smaller, a window rather than a horizon, because everywhere else there is work to do — but here you are allowed the whole of it.

*Never here — the orchard turned into wallpaper pasted behind everything; the view forced to carry text or work it was never meant to hold; anything in it moving to be noticed.*
*What lingers — the calm of a world that is always there, asking nothing, promising to keep.*

## Tea Before The Day Begins

This is the unhurried in-between: kettle on, the day not quite claimed yet, a few minutes that belong to no task. You are checking in, not working — a glance to see how the household is doing and what is coming, the way you might read the light and the weather of your own morning before deciding anything.

The atmosphere is low and warm and entirely without urgency. Nothing flashes. Nothing counts down. If there is something worth knowing, it is offered the way a friend slides a mug across the counter — here, no rush, drink it while it's warm. If there is nothing worth saying, the house has the confidence to stay quiet, and quiet here never curdles into cold. The morning light is soft on the surfaces; the orchard is a warm presence at the edge of things rather than the centre of them, because your attention is inward, on your own household, on the shape of the day.

The Companion is at its best in this moment: present, prepared, and content to wait. It has thought about dinner already, so you don't have to yet. If you ask, it answers plainly and puts back what it borrowed of your attention. If you don't, it simply keeps you company. It never fills the silence to seem useful.

*Never here — a notification breaking the calm to justify itself; the quiet mistaken for emptiness and filled with noise; the Companion performing helpfulness instead of simply being ready.*
*What lingers — the small luxury of a few minutes that were yours, and the sense that the day is already half-thought-through on your behalf.*

## The Family Planning Table

Now there is work, and it is the good kind — the kind a family does together around a table rather than the kind a person fills in alone. You are laying out the week: what you'll eat, who's in, what to cook. It feels less like a form and more like spreading things out on a big clear surface where everyone can see and everyone can reach.

The mood is unhurried control. The whole week sits on one solid, well-lit surface, steady under your hand — a table, not a stack of drawers you have to keep opening. Today has a gentle warmth on it, half a degree brighter than the rest, so you always know where "now" is without being told. Decisions feel makeable and unmakeable; nothing is heavier than it needs to be. The light here is even and working — the plain, honest light you'd want over a real table — and the orchard has stepped back to being warmth and daylight through the room rather than a view, because the work is the point and the work deserves the room.

The Companion sits with you the way a knowledgeable friend sits at the table: it might suggest, it might notice you're short a night or repeating a meal, but it never reaches over and decides for you. The choice stays yours, every time. The materials stay plain and generous so the plan can breathe; when there is more to consider, the table gives you more room, it never crowds the week to fit.

*Never here — the table turned into a form to be completed; the plan crowded until it feels like a chore; the Companion making the family's choices for them; a decorative view stealing room the week needs to think.*
*What lingers — the feeling of a week gently gathered and under control, decided together, with time left over for the eating.*

## The Living Cookbook

You take down the book that is *yours* — not a catalogue of every dish in the world, but the well-used one, the family's own, the recipes you actually cook with the corners softened from use. Opening it feels like reaching for a familiar shelf, not browsing a shop. The pleasure is recognition: *oh, this one*, and the small warmth of a page that has clearly been opened before.

The room is warm and calm and made for lingering. Side-light falls across the page the way afternoon light falls across a book left open on a kitchen table. Through a framed window the orchard is present but content to be at the edge, one committed corner of the room that the pages never cover — a partial, quiet company rather than a backdrop. The materials are the materials of a real book and a real shelf: things you pick up, set down, come back to. The ones you cook most often carry a faint, earned warmth, the way a loved page falls open on its own — never a badge, never a rank, never a score. This book keeps no leaderboard. It simply shows, quietly, that it is used.

The Companion is the friend leaning on the counter while you leaf through — happy to say "you loved this in the winter" or "this one's quick tonight" if you want it, happy to stay quiet while you decide. It never turns the browsing into a task or the book into a store.

*Never here — a drawn book with turning-page theatrics; a streak or a rank competing with the food; the family's own cookbook made to feel like a catalogue of everything they haven't cooked.*
*What lingers — the warmth of a book that is theirs, well-loved and unhurried, that makes cooking feel like remembering rather than choosing.*

## The Pantry Shelves

You open the cupboard to see, honestly, what is in the house right now. Not what you should have. Not what you've run out of, held up as a failing. Just the plain truth of the shelves, told kindly — what's here, what's fresh, what's getting on. It is the most practical moment in the house, and its whole grace is honesty without guilt.

The feeling is reassurance. Nothing here scolds you. Freshness is told in helpful, human words, never in a red warning or a wagging finger — a thing to know, not a thing to feel bad about. The light is practical and morning-warm, the light you'd want to actually see the shelves by. The orchard is at its smallest here, the narrowest window in the house, because this is a working cupboard and the work is looking, checking, knowing. The materials are the strata of a real pantry — tiers, shelves, things in their places — plain and solid and legible.

The Companion, if it speaks, is the friend who glances over your shoulder and says something *useful* — "you've got most of tomorrow's already" — never something that makes you feel you've been caught out. It helps you see; it never judges what it sees.

*Never here — painted jars or invented fullness pretending the cupboard is more than it is; freshness turned into shame; the household made to feel it is failing at its own kitchen.*
*What lingers — the quiet reassurance of knowing exactly what's in the house, told plainly and kindly, with nothing to feel guilty about.*

## Preparing To Leave For The Shop

This is a room you pass through on your way out the door, not one you settle into. The list is gathering by the door — the things you need, ready to go. The whole feeling is brisk and satisfying, the small competence of being organised on the way out: keys, bag, list, done.

There is momentum here, and it is welcome. Items settle onto the list; when something's handled it strikes through and slips quietly into the background, out of the way but not gone — the small satisfaction of a thing crossed off, without a fanfare for each one. The light is plain and bright, the light of a hallway you move through rather than a room you dwell in. The orchard is barely a thought here — a working brightness — though on a clear day, when the list is short and honest, the room can afford to breathe a little and let a bit more of the morning in. The materials are as simple as a note by the door, sized to exactly what's on it and no more.

The Companion is the friend who catches you at the threshold — "you're out of the thing you use every week," offered once, easy to take or leave. It helps you leave prepared; it never holds you at the door.

*Never here — confetti or celebration for finishing a shopping list; a grand landscape behind a handful of items; the quick room turned into a place that asks you to linger.*
*What lingers — the light, competent feeling of heading out prepared, with everything you need gathered and nothing forgotten.*

## Returning Home

You come back. Maybe you were gone a minute, maybe a week. It does not matter — the house is exactly as you left it, the same warm morning, the same still orchard, the same quiet welcome. Returning is not re-orientation; there is nothing to relearn. The walls are where the walls were. Home is one step away, as it always is. You are simply back.

The feeling is the particular relief of a place that did not change under you. So much of a day changes — this did not. You cross the threshold and you are oriented in a glance: how things are, what's next, all of it held steady in your absence. The light is the morning you remember; the orchard is where you left it; the surfaces are the same honest planes. Nothing has been rearranged to seem fresh. Nothing greets you differently to prove it's been busy.

The Companion is there again, in the same chair at the counter, arriving a beat after you the way it always does — not pouncing with everything that happened while you were out, just present again, ready again. If something genuinely needs you, it is offered calmly. Otherwise it simply keeps you company, glad you're back without making a performance of it.

*Never here — the house rearranged to look new since you left; a pile of everything-that-happened dumped on you at the door; being made to find your bearings again in your own home.*
*What lingers — the deep ease of a place that kept, that you can leave and come back to and always find waiting, unchanged and warm.*

## Cooking Together

This is the moment the whole house is *for*. Not the planning of the meal, not the shopping for it — the making of it, together, in the warm middle of the evening. THA is not in your hands now; it has already done its part and stepped back. What it gave you was the easy afternoon, the thought-through week, the honest cupboard — and now the kitchen is yours and the technology has quietly disappeared, exactly as it should.

The feeling is presence: hands busy, people close, the good ordinary noise of a meal coming together. If the house is anywhere in this moment, it is a calm reference at the edge — a step you needed, a quantity you forgot — offered instantly and then out of the way again. It never stands between you and the food. The light is the same steady morning it always is, warm on the working surface. The orchard is a settled presence beyond the glass, the timeless thing this timeless act happens beside — a family eating well together, as old as families, framed by an orchard as old as orchards.

The Companion is the friend who stays useful and knows when to be quiet — there if you call, gone from your attention when you don't. It never narrates your cooking. It never inserts itself into the warmest part of the evening to be noticed. Its finest moment is the one where you forget it is there at all.

*Never here — the technology becoming the subject of the evening; the Companion performing in the middle of a family meal; a screen competing with the people in the room.*
*What lingers — the feeling of having been helped so well that the help disappeared, leaving only the meal, the people, and the time to enjoy them.*

## Quiet Moments

Not every visit has a task. Sometimes you come to the house simply to be in it for a minute — to check, to note something, to sit for a moment in a calm corner before moving on. The house has room for this. It is not a machine that only runs when you feed it work; it is a place, and places let you stand in them doing nothing in particular.

The feeling is stillness that is warm rather than empty. There is a difference, and the house knows it: the quiet of a room someone keeps, not the quiet of a room no one uses. Nothing is happening, and nothing needs to. The light is gentle and gathered; the orchard is softly present; the surfaces hold the most air in the house here, generous and unfilled on purpose. Emptiness that feels tended is calm. Emptiness that feels abandoned is cold — and this house is never cold.

The Companion honours the quiet. It does not read stillness as a cue to start talking. It is the friend comfortable enough to sit with you in silence, present but not filling the air. When you have something small to leave — a line, a note, a trace of the day — the house holds it plainly and hands it back the next time, only when it's true, never analysed back at you unasked.

*Never here — calm curdling into cold or lifeless; the room dimmed to signal "reflective" — quieter is never darker; the silence filled with noise to seem alive.*
*What lingers — the rare comfort of a place that is content to be still with you, warm even when nothing is happening.*

## Rain Against The Glass

Some days you come to the house from a hard one. It is raining in *your* world — a long day, a short temper, the ordinary weather of a life. You open THA carrying it. And the house is the same warm morning it always is. The rain is on your side of the glass, never inside. The house does not perform sympathy or change its face to match your mood; it simply stays warm, and steady, and unbothered, and lets you in.

This is the deepest thing the constancy is for. A house that changed with your weather would be one more thing to manage; a house that keeps is a shelter. The orchard beyond the window does not gloom over to mirror your day — it stays bright and in leaf and alive, and its refusal to darken is not indifference, it is the whole comfort. There is somewhere that is still fine, still morning, still tended, no matter what kind of day you had. The materials are the same honest, warm surfaces; the light is the same low gold. Nothing has curled up because you are tired.

The Companion, on a day like this, is the friend who doesn't make it worse — plain, kind, low, useful if you want it and invisible if you don't. It never brightens artificially to cheer you up, never floods you with encouragement, never asks how you're feeling. It just quietly helps you get dinner sorted and leaves you the space to be human.

*Never here — the house darkening or dramatising to match a hard mood; forced cheer thrown at a tired person; the orchard turned gloomy, misty, or melancholy to seem sympathetic.*
*What lingers — the shelter of a place that stayed warm when your day didn't, and asked nothing of you when you had nothing to give.*

## Spring

The family's year turns, even though the house's morning does not. Spring, for the household, is a particular energy — lighter meals, longer light in *their* world, a certain lift after a heavy season. They bring that spring in with them. The house does not put on blossom to greet it; the orchard does not suddenly flower. What is springlike is in the *family's* life — what they feel like eating, the quickening of their weeks — and the house simply holds it, the way a good kitchen holds every season of a family's cooking without redecorating for each one.

The feeling, in this chapter of the household's year, is renewal met by constancy. The lightness is theirs; the steadiness is the house's, and the two sit well together. The light in the house is the same morning it has always been — because the household's spring does not need the house to pretend it is spring; it needs the house to be the reliable warm place their spring happens beside. The orchard is what it always is: mature, in leaf, alive — timeless precisely so that the family's changing year has something unchanging to turn against.

The Companion notices the household's real rhythms, not the calendar's costume — that they're reaching for quicker, fresher things, that the weeks have a new shape — and quietly follows their life, never a seasonal theme. Its care shows in understanding *them*, not in dressing the house for a date.

*Never here — the house or the orchard putting on spring dressing; blossom, pastel, or seasonal decoration mistaken for warmth; the constancy of the house treated as something to interrupt for novelty.*
*What lingers — the sense that the household's own spring was met and understood, beside a house calm enough to make the season feel like theirs, not the app's.*

## Summer

Summer is the household's fullest season — long days, people around, food that is casual and generous and often. Their kitchen is busy in a happy way. They arrive at the house more often, in shorter bursts, with more mouths to feed and less patience for fuss. The house takes all of it without heating up. It stays the same measured morning, the same calm surface, however full the family's summer gets.

The feeling here is abundance held lightly. The household's life is at its most plentiful; the house's job is to keep that plenty easy, never to add to the noise of a full season. The light is the steady morning it always is — the household's summer glare stays outside, in their world; inside is the even, honest warmth that makes a busy week feel manageable. The orchard beyond the glass is in its timeless fullness, and for once the family's season and the orchard's constancy rhyme — both generous, both alive — though the orchard is generous *always*, not just now, and that is the quiet reminder underneath: this fullness is available every season, not only the bright one.

The Companion, in the busy season, is the friend who keeps the plates spinning without comment — more meals, more people, more repetition, all absorbed calmly, all made lighter. It never celebrates the busyness or turns the household's fullest weeks into a performance of how much it is doing.

*Never here — the house amplifying a busy season into overwhelm; the Companion showing off how much it's handling; the plenty of summer used as an excuse to clutter the calm.*
*What lingers — the ease of a full, generous season made to feel light, beside a house that never got flustered no matter how busy the family got.*

## Autumn

Autumn, in the family's life, is a drawing-in — heartier food, earlier evenings in *their* world, a return to routine after the loose summer. There is a comfort to it, and a slight melancholy that belongs to the household, not to the house. They bring the turning of their year through the door. The house does not turn with them. No leaves fall in here; the orchard does not go amber or bare. The house stays the one warm morning, and that refusal to fade is a kindness: as the family's days get shorter and cooler, the house stays exactly as bright and warm as it was in June.

The feeling is comfort against a drawing-in. The household is battening down a little; the house is the steady, lit, warm place they batten down *toward*. The light inside stays golden and low and unchanged — because the household's autumn does not need the house to dim in sympathy; it needs the house to be the warm room at the end of a shorter day. The orchard holds its mature, in-leaf fullness, timeless as ever, and its constancy is felt most sharply exactly now, when everything in the family's own world is turning.

The Companion follows the household into heartier, homelier cooking — noticing the shift toward comfort, the return of routine — as a quiet understanding of their life, never as an autumn theme laid over the house. It reads the family's season in what they cook, not in a calendar.

*Never here — falling leaves, harvest dressing, or amber light brought into the house; the orchard allowed to wither or turn; the house dimming to mirror the shortening of the family's days.*
*What lingers — the comfort of a warm, unchanging room to draw in toward, that stayed bright while the family's own year began to close.*

## Winter

Winter is the household's hardest, darkest season — cold outside, long evenings, the pull to stay in and eat warm and simple. They come to the house tired and early and often, out of the dark of *their* world. And the house is the one warm morning it has always been. This is where the whole design of the house pays off most: in the depth of the family's winter, there is a place that is still bright, still in leaf, still tended, still expecting them — a perpetual morning that the season cannot reach.

The feeling is refuge. Everything in the household's world is telling them it is winter; the house, quietly and without argument, keeps a different weather. It does not gloat about being warm and it does not pretend the family's winter isn't real — it simply *is* warm, and lets that be enough. The light stays low and gold and full; the orchard stays alive beyond the glass, and its aliveness in the dead of the family's winter is the most reassuring sight in the house. Something is still growing. Something is still fine. The materials are the same honest warm surfaces they always were; nothing has gone cold to match the season.

The Companion, in the family's hardest season, is the friend who makes the warm simple things easy — the hearty meal, the short list, the plan that doesn't ask much of a tired household. It stays low and kind and useful, and never brightens falsely to fight the season on the family's behalf. Its warmth is real because it is steady, not because it is turned up.

*Never here — the house going cold, dark, or bare to reflect winter; snow, frost, or seasonal dressing on the orchard; the family's hardest season made harder by a house that changed under them.*
*What lingers — the refuge of a warm, morning-lit place that kept a different weather than the world outside, and was still growing when nothing else seemed to be.*

## Evening In The Orchard

The household's own day is ending. They come to the house late, the dishes done, the house — *their* house — going quiet. This is their evening. And here, in THA, it is still morning, still warm, still bright, and that is not a contradiction — it is the point. "Evening in the orchard" is the household's evening spent beside a timeless orchard that keeps no clock. The family's day closes; the orchard does not. They rest against something that stays.

The feeling is a settled, completed calm — the calm of a day put to bed, spent in the company of a place that will still be exactly here tomorrow. There is no wind-down performance, no dimming of lights to say "it's late now," no goodnight from the house. Quieter is never darker here; the house does not perform evening any more than it performs weather. The light stays the low warm gold it always is. The orchard stands beyond the glass in its perpetual morning, and at the end of a long day there is something profoundly restful about a view that will not have changed by dawn.

The Companion, at the close of the day, is the friend who knows the evening belongs to the household and not to it. It does not follow them into their night with reminders or reflections. If tomorrow is thought-through, it lets that be quietly true and says nothing. It has already done its work; now it simply keeps the last of the company, and lets the household leave calmer than they came.

*Never here — the house performing dusk or bedtime; a wind-down ritual manufactured to seem thoughtful; the Companion trailing the household into the end of their day; the orchard fading toward night.*
*What lingers — the deep rest of ending the day beside something that keeps, and leaving the house — as the household should leave every visit — a little calmer than they came.*

## The Orchard Promise

Every chapter of this book is one house, seen at a different hour of a family's life, and every chapter makes the same quiet promise. Written once, plainly, as the thing every future THA experience exists to protect:

> **The Healthy Apples will always be a warm, lived-in home on one unchanging morning, where an ancient orchard keeps outside every window, and where the household is always the most present thing in the room.**
>
> It will expect you before it asks anything of you. It will stay the same warm morning through every season of your life, every kind of day, every mood you arrive carrying — so that whatever changes in your world, one place kept. It will help you so well that the help disappears, and leave you holding only the meal, the people, and the time. It will decorate itself with nothing but the evidence that your family lives here. It will never perform, never fabricate a feeling, never make its own cleverness the subject, and never make you feel less capable in your own kitchen. And it will let you leave, every single time, a little calmer than you came.

Whatever is built next — a new room, a new season of the product, a capability no one has imagined yet — is finished only when a household could live through the moments in this book inside it and feel every one of them kept. **Protect the promise, and you have protected the house.**
