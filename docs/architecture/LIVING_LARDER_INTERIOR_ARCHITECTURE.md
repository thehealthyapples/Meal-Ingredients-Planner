# PART I — THE ROOM

*Interior architecture, described independently of software. The seven questions the brief asks of every storage area — why it exists, what belongs here, how it eases family life, how it reduces effort, how it encourages healthier habits, how it feels welcoming — are answered zone by zone in § I.5.*

## I.1 The idea of the room

**The Larder is a real household larder, not a list** (`LARDER1` § 1, cited — the North Star this room realises).

A household knows its own larder as a *place*. You do not audit it; you *look* at it. You know the flour is getting low because the jar is nearly down to the last cup, not because a number told you so. You know where the tinned tomatoes live because they have always lived there. The larder is not a database of your cupboards — it is your cupboards, kept and known.

This document designs that place completely: **the room comes first and is permanent; the products simply live within it.** The furniture is designed before the food. The storage philosophy is designed before the products. Only then do real jars, tins, bottles, baskets and packets populate an architecture that was already there to receive them.

This is also why it is called the **Living Larder**. It belongs to the household's Living Home (`LIVINGHOME1`, cited), and it obeys that home's one law: **the house holds still; the life moves** (`LIVINGHOME1` § — the Living Home Principle, cited). The room — the shelving, the wings, the light — never changes under the family. What changes is only ever *their own life shown truthfully*: what they keep, what is running low, what they have taken out and put back. The architecture is the constant the family returns to; their provisions are the life that moves through it.

## I.2 The room as you walk in

Before any shelf is named, the room must be felt as a whole.

You enter a **warm, sunlit larder** — cream and honey, oak and stone, one morning of light falling from the upper left exactly as it falls in every room of this house (Kept Room Translation § 4.1 *Light* — "one morning sun, upper-left, one hour, in every room forever"; cited through to the Experience Blueprint § 7, its owner). The air is a **building material here, not leftover room** (Kept Room Translation § 4.1 *Space*, cited): the shelves breathe, the surfaces are calm, and where there is nothing to store there is light and quiet rather than clutter. It should feel, in one sentence, like *"a warm, lived-in home where someone has already thought about dinner"* (Experience Language § 3A.2, cited).

The room is organised the way a good family larder is organised: **the things you reach for daily are at hand; the things you reach for rarely are kept but out of the way; cold things are cold; dry things are dry; and everything has a place it lives, so you find it by memory, not by searching.** You do not read this room. You recognise it. The feeling the room must produce, at the threshold, is not *"here is my inventory"* — it is ***"that is my larder."*** (`LARDER1` § 1, the Experience Test, cited.)

Nothing in the room asks to be maintained. There is no audit to complete, no row to tick, no count to reconcile. A more capable larder is a *quieter* one (`GEA2`, cited): the room's job is to *hold* the family's provisions well, so that the family carries less — not to give them a second job of keeping the larder's records straight.

## I.3 The organising principle — how the room is laid out, and why

A family larder is not one undifferentiated cupboard. It is a small suite of **wings**, each answering a different rhythm of family life. The Living Larder is laid out as six wings, arranged in the order a household actually moves through a kitchen — dry heart first, daily rhythm at hand, cold store to one side, the occasional and the working and the growing further out:

| Wing | What it is for | The rhythm it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **A · The Dry Store** (the heart) | The great body of keeping staples — everything shelf-stable | *"What do we always have in?"* |
| **B · The Daily Rhythm** | The few stations touched every single morning and evening | *"What starts and ends our day?"* |
| **C · The Cool Store** | Everything that must be kept cold or fresh | *"What won't keep on a shelf?"* |
| **D · Hospitality** | What the home keeps ready for guests and gatherings | *"What do we reach for when people come?"* |
| **E · The Working House** | The non-food provisions a running home needs | *"What keeps the house itself going?"* |
| **F · The Seasonal & Growing Room** | What comes and goes with the year, and room reserved for what the family will keep in future | *"What is this season, and what is coming?"* |

The wings exist **because family life has these rhythms, not because food has these labels.** This is the load-bearing design decision of the whole room, and it is why the Larder is a place and not a taxonomy: you do not sort your kitchen by botanical family; you sort it by *when and how you reach for things.* Breakfast is a rhythm, so it is a station, not a food group. Baking is an occasion, so it is an area you return to whole. Cold is a physical fact, so it is a wing of its own. The organising principle is **human use, arranged in space** — and everything below follows from it.

## I.4 The permanent architecture (furniture first)

The furniture is fixed. It is the room's bones, and it is there whether the shelves are full or nearly bare. Designing it first — before a single product — is what makes the room permanent: a family can grow into it and out of it for years, and the room itself never has to be rebuilt.

**The fixed elements of the Living Larder:**

- **Open shelving** — the ordered strata a household stores and finds things on (Kept Room Translation § 4.2 *Shelving*, cited): tiers you read at a glance, legible *because they breathe, not because they are packed.* Solidity descends with importance — the daily strata at hand, the rarely-touched higher or deeper. This is the dominant surface of the Dry Store.
- **Cupboards** — enclosed keeping for what does not want to be on show: the household cupboard, the overflow of the dry store, the things kept but not displayed.
- **Deep drawers** — for the low, heavy, and loose: bags of flour and rice, potatoes and onions, the odds and ends. A drawer is read by pulling it toward you, so its contents are seen from above, all at once.
- **Pull-out storage** — narrow, tall runs beside the working surfaces: the oils, the vinegars, the bottles that are used often and want to be reached without moving anything.
- **Baskets** — for loose produce and things that breathe: onions, garlic, potatoes, fruit. Woven, open, honest — you see the whole basket and know at a glance what is in it.
- **A spice rack** — shallow, at eye level, by where the cooking happens: many small things, each found by sight because it is where it always is.
- **A counter / working surface** — warm oak, the one place in the room that is a working surface before it is anything else (Kept Room Translation § 4.2 *Oak*, cited): the baking area and the still point of the room.
- **The cold appliances** — the **fridge** and the **freezer**, each a door you open onto compartments and shelves.
- **Reserved bays** — deliberately unfilled runs of shelving the room keeps in hand, so it can grow (§ I.9) without redesign.

Every one of these is a *presentation* — a way of showing and touching provisions — and **owns no fact by existing** (§ II.10). An empty spice rack is still a spice rack. That is the point of designing the furniture first.

## I.5 The complete interior — a walk through the wings

*Each storage area below answers the brief's seven questions — **why it exists · what naturally belongs · how the family uses it · how it reduces effort · how it encourages healthier habits · how it feels welcoming** — and names **the form the products take** there (jars, tins, bottles, packets, boxes, baskets, loose produce). The mapping of each area to the facts THA owns is set out once, cleanly, in § II.11 — so this walk stays a walk through a room.*

### Wing A — The Dry Store (the heart of the larder)

The largest wing, on open shelving. It holds everything shelf-stable, and it is the wing a household means when it says *"the larder."* Within it, the household's eye finds smaller, familiar areas — not because anyone labelled them, but because like lives with like.

**A1 · Dry goods — the body of the store**
- **Why it exists.** Every household keeps a core of shelf-stable staples — the flour, rice, pasta, pulses, grains, sugar, tinned goods that turn into most meals. This is the larder's centre of gravity.
- **What belongs.** Bags and jars of flour, rice and grains; dried pasta; lentils and beans; sugar; stock; the everyday tins.
- **How the family uses it.** Reached daily, cooked from nightly. Read at a glance to answer *"can I make something tonight?"* before a shop is even considered.
- **How it reduces effort.** A full dry store is a week of dinners already half-decided. Seeing it full is seeing that the household is provisioned.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Whole grains, pulses and dried staples sit at the front and full where a household can build meals around them — the plant-diverse, less-processed core is the part of the kitchen most in view. (The room *shows* this; it never scores it — `GEA13`, cited.)
- **How it feels welcoming.** This is the shelf that says *the house is kept.* A full larder is one of the oldest images of a home that is looked after.
- **Product forms.** **Jars and large jars** (flour, rice, pasta, sugar, grains — decanted and read by fullness); **packets and boxes** (pasta, cereals in wait); **tins** (stacked, read by presence). *(See A2, A3.)*

**A2 · Jars and large jars — the decanted staples**
- **Why it exists.** Decanting into jars is how a household turns a shelf of packaging into a shelf it can *read.* A glass jar shows its own level; a cardboard box does not.
- **What belongs.** The bulk dry staples a family goes through steadily: flour, sugar, rice, oats, pasta, pulses, coffee beans, loose tea.
- **How the family uses it.** Read by *level.* A full jar and a nearly-empty jar are two different facts a family reads without thinking — this is the room's primary way of showing availability (§ I.8).
- **How it reduces effort.** No counting. The jar *is* the measure — a fuller jar, a lower jar — and that is all a household needs to know whether to add it to the shop.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Whole ingredients in clear jars are more inviting to cook from than sealed packets pushed to the back; visibility favours the wholefood.
- **How it feels welcoming.** A row of matched jars, each holding a different staple at its own honest level, is the warm, ordered image the whole room is built around.
- **Product forms.** **Jars** (standard) and **large jars** (bulk staples). Read by fullness, never by number.

**A3 · The tinned shelf**
- **Why it exists.** Tins are the household's reserve — the tomatoes, beans, fish, coconut milk, chopped fruit that keep for a year and turn a bare cupboard into a meal.
- **What belongs.** Tinned tomatoes, beans and pulses, tinned fish, coconut milk, tinned vegetables and fruit.
- **How the family uses it.** Stacked at the back and read by *presence* — *do we have tomatoes in?* — rather than by exact count.
- **How it reduces effort.** The tinned shelf is the answer to *"there's nothing to eat."* Seeing it stocked removes a whole category of daily worry.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Tinned pulses and fish are among the cheapest routes to protein and fibre; kept visible, they are reached for.
- **How it feels welcoming.** The quiet security of a stocked reserve — the shelf you are glad is there on a tired evening.
- **Product forms.** **Tins** — read by presence, stacked; availability is *have / running low / out*, never a tally.

**A4 · The baking area**
- **Why it exists.** Baking is an occasion a household returns to *whole* — you do not gather flour, sugar, butter and vanilla from six different places; you keep them together because you use them together.
- **What belongs.** Flour, sugar (caster, icing, brown), baking powder and bicarbonate, vanilla and extracts, cocoa, dried fruit, chocolate, sprinkles, paper cases.
- **How the family uses it.** Reached as a set, on the weekend or the birthday. A baking area kept together means *the intention to bake is never defeated by the search.*
- **How it reduces effort.** Everything for a bake in one reach. The activation cost of baking with children drops to almost nothing.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Home baking is baking a household controls — sugar, portion and ingredient. An inviting baking area is a gentle argument for the home-made over the packaged.
- **How it feels welcoming.** This is the warmest corner of the room — the smell of a Sunday, the thing children remember.
- **Product forms.** **Jars** (flour, sugar, cocoa), **packets** (dried fruit, chocolate), **small bottles** (extracts), **boxes** (cases, decorations).

**A5 · The spice rack**
- **Why it exists.** Spices are many small things, each used in a pinch, each found by sight. A rack keeps them shallow and at eye level so the whole collection is read at once.
- **What belongs.** Dried herbs and ground spices, whole spices, seeds, salt and peppercorns, the small jars of the world's kitchens.
- **How the family uses it.** By the hob, mid-cook, without breaking stride. Found by position — the cumin is where the cumin lives.
- **How it reduces effort.** A visible spice rack removes the mid-recipe scramble and the *"do we even have paprika?"* — and prevents the quiet waste of buying a third jar of something already owned twice.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** A good spice collection is what makes vegetables and pulses worth eating — flavour is the difference between the healthy meal cooked and the healthy meal abandoned. A full rack quietly widens what a household will cook.
- **How it feels welcoming.** Colour, variety, the sense of a cook's kitchen — a spice rack is the most personal shelf in the room.
- **Product forms.** **Small jars** — read by presence and, loosely, by fullness. Never counted.

**A6 · Oils and vinegars**
- **Why it exists.** The bottles a household cooks and dresses with, used constantly, wanting to stand upright and near the working surface.
- **What belongs.** Olive and cooking oils, vinegars (balsamic, wine, cider), soy and other sauces that live in bottles.
- **How the family uses it.** Reached daily, poured by eye. Kept in a pull-out or a run beside the counter so the everyday oil is never behind anything.
- **How it reduces effort.** The most-reached bottles at the most-reached place; nothing to move to get to them.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** A good oil and a good vinegar are what make a salad or a plate of vegetables a pleasure — kept at hand, they are used.
- **How it feels welcoming.** The bottles are among the most beautiful objects in any kitchen; a lit row of oils and vinegars is a small luxury the whole household sees.
- **Product forms.** **Bottles** — standing, read by level, refilled or replaced when low.

**A7 · Preserves**
- **Why it exists.** The jams, honeys, marmalades, chutneys, pickles and nut butters — the sweet and sharp keeping-things a household spreads, spoons and serves.
- **What belongs.** Jam and marmalade, honey and syrups, peanut and nut butters, chutney and pickle, preserved lemons and the like.
- **How the family uses it.** At breakfast and at the cheese board; opened jars migrate to the fridge, sealed jars keep on the shelf.
- **How it reduces effort.** A kept shelf of preserves means the breakfast table and the impromptu ploughman's are always possible without a shop.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Home and small-batch preserves, kept and valued, are a route away from the ultra-processed spread — and the shelf is where a household's own preserving (§ I.9) will one day live.
- **How it feels welcoming.** A row of preserves is a picture of plenty and of a home that keeps things — the shelf most likely to hold something made, or gifted, or remembered.
- **Product forms.** **Jars** — read by presence and fullness; the great natural home of the *making* the room will grow into.

### Wing B — The Daily Rhythm

The two stations a household touches every single day, kept apart from the great dry store because their rhythm is different: not *"what can we cook?"* but *"what starts and ends the day?"*

**B1 · The breakfast area**
- **Why it exists.** Breakfast is the one meal reached for half-awake, at speed, by everyone. It deserves its own station so the morning is never a search.
- **What belongs.** Cereals, oats and granola, spreads that live on the shelf, breakfast preserves, the tea and coffee beside it (B2).
- **How the family uses it.** Every morning, fast, often by children. A breakfast station is *the morning made calm.*
- **How it reduces effort.** Everything for the first meal in one place — the single biggest daily reduction in kitchen friction a larder can offer.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Oats, whole cereals and fruit at the front of the breakfast station make the good breakfast the easy one; the household's own choices, kept in view.
- **How it feels welcoming.** The first shelf of the day should feel like a soft landing, not a scramble. This is the room greeting the morning.
- **Product forms.** **Boxes and packets** (cereals), **large jars** (oats, granola — decanted, read by level), with the preserves of A7 and the drinks of B2 close by.

**B2 · The tea and coffee station**
- **Why it exists.** The most-repeated ritual in most homes. Tea and coffee want to be together, near the kettle, always found in the same reach.
- **What belongs.** Tea (bags and loose), coffee (beans, ground, pods), hot chocolate, sugar and sweeteners, the things that go in a hot drink.
- **How the family uses it.** Many times a day, by habit, often for a guest within a minute of them arriving (this station is the quiet hinge between the family's rhythm and the home's hospitality — Wing D).
- **How it reduces effort.** A fixed station means the drink is made without thought — the definition of a well-kept small ritual.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Keeping the good coffee and the herbal and decaf teas visible and stocked is a gentle nudge; the station's calm is itself part of a household's wellbeing.
- **How it feels welcoming.** *"Can I get you a tea?"* is the first sentence of hospitality in most homes; the station that answers it is the warmest utility in the room.
- **Product forms.** **Boxes and caddies** (tea), **jars and bags** (coffee, beans) — read by presence and fullness.

### Wing C — The Cool Store

Everything that will not keep on a dry shelf. A physical fact, so a wing of its own.

**C1 · The produce baskets (the cool larder)**
- **Why it exists.** Fruit and the vegetables that keep out of the fridge — potatoes, onions, garlic, squash, bananas, citrus — want a cool, dark, airy place, not a cold one.
- **What belongs.** Loose fruit and root vegetables; onions, garlic, potatoes, squash; the bowl of whatever is in season.
- **How the family uses it.** Reached for constantly, chosen by eye and hand, replenished from the weekly shop.
- **How it reduces effort.** Produce kept where it is seen is produce that is eaten before it turns; out of sight is the road to waste.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** This is the single most important wing for how a household eats. Fresh produce, kept **at eye level and in the light**, is the room quietly making the fruit bowl the easiest thing to reach. (This is *the household's own produce, data-borne* — see the firm distinction in § II.12: the room shows the family's real fruit, and never decorates itself with a bowl of apples it invented — `LIVINGHOME2` § 5.1 / `ED3`, cited.)
- **How it feels welcoming.** A basket of fruit and a string of onions is the most alive image in a kitchen — colour, freshness, the orchard brought indoors.
- **Product forms.** **Baskets** and **loose produce** — read as *have / running low* by looking; open, honest, never a count.

**C2 · The fridge**
- **Why it exists.** Cold keeping — dairy, fresh proteins, opened jars, leftovers, the things that spoil at room temperature.
- **What belongs.** Milk, butter, cheese, eggs, yoghurt; fresh meat and fish; opened preserves and sauces; salad and herbs; leftovers.
- **How the family uses it.** Opened many times a day, read by door and shelf. Organised as compartments — a door of bottles, shelves of the everyday, a drawer of fresh.
- **How it reduces effort.** A known fridge is a fridge whose contents you carry in your head; seeing it confirms or corrects that picture in a glance.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Fresh proteins, dairy and vegetables kept visible and used in time; the room helps the household see what needs eating *without ever tracking a date it does not own* (§ II.12 — Domain 30 owns no time; `LARDER1` § 3, cited).
- **How it feels welcoming.** The most-opened door in the house; a well-kept fridge is the daily proof that the family is fed.
- **Product forms.** **Bottles and cartons** (in the door), **tubs and packets** (on the shelves), **loose fresh** (in the drawer) — availability read by looking, never by a best-before the room does not own.

**C3 · The freezer**
- **Why it exists.** Long cold keeping — the household's deeper reserve of frozen produce, proteins, bread, and the batch-cooked and leftover meals that are the busy-week safety net.
- **What belongs.** Frozen vegetables and fruit, frozen fish and meat, bread, ice, and home-frozen meals and portions.
- **How the family uses it.** Reached into for the meal there was no time to cook fresh; organised by drawer and compartment.
- **How it reduces effort.** The freezer is the answer to the worst evenings. A stocked freezer is a household that is never truly stuck.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Frozen vegetables and fish are as nutritious as fresh and never wasted; a good freezer is a quiet route to the home-cooked meal over the takeaway.
- **How it feels welcoming.** The deep reassurance of a reserve — the wing you are most grateful for when life is hard.
- **Product forms.** **Bags and boxes** (frozen produce), **wrapped portions** (proteins, bread), **home-frozen containers** (the household's own batch cooking) — read by presence, never by a countdown.

### Wing D — Hospitality

What a home keeps ready for other people. It exists because **hospitality is the founding value of the whole house** (`GEA1` — hospitality before productivity; `CRAFT1` § 6, cited), and a larder that could not welcome a guest would have missed its purpose.

**D1 · The hospitality store**
- **Why it exists.** Every household keeps a small reserve for when people come — the nice things not eaten on an ordinary Tuesday, held ready so a guest is never met with an empty cupboard.
- **What belongs.** Crackers and biscuits for cheese, nice chocolate, nuts and olives, the good coffee, condiments and antipasti kept for a board.
- **How the family uses it.** Reached for on short notice — the drop-in visitor, the Sunday guest, the celebration that was not planned a week ahead.
- **How it reduces effort.** Hospitality-on-hand removes the panic shop. *Someone is coming* becomes a pleasure, not a scramble.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** A considered hospitality store — nuts, olives, good crackers, real chocolate — is a kinder default than the emergency dash for the ultra-processed.
- **How it feels welcoming.** This wing *is* welcome, kept in a cupboard. It is the household's readiness to receive people, made physical.
- **Product forms.** **Boxes and packets** (crackers, biscuits), **jars** (olives, antipasti), **bottles** (see D2).

**D2 · Drinks**
- **Why it exists.** The bottles and cartons a household keeps for the table and for guests — soft drinks, juices, cordials, and, where the home keeps them, wine and beer for hospitality.
- **What belongs.** Juices and cordials, sparkling and soft drinks, mixers; the household's kept bottles for guests and occasions.
- **How the family uses it.** Reached for at the table, at gatherings, when guests arrive; a store that is topped up before an occasion, not during it.
- **How it reduces effort.** Drinks kept ready mean the table is set without a special trip.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Keeping water, sparkling water and low-sugar options visible and stocked makes them the easy pour; the room lays the choices out honestly and lets the household decide (`GEA23`, cited).
- **How it feels welcoming.** A drink offered is hospitality's first gesture; a kept drinks store is the home ready to make it.
- **Product forms.** **Bottles and cartons** — read by presence; a natural early candidate for a wing of its own as the room grows (§ I.9).

### Wing E — The Working House

The non-food provisions a running home needs. The larder is not only a food store; a real household cupboard keeps the things that *keep the house going,* and a room that pretended a family only stores food would be a smaller, less honest room.

**E1 · The household store**
- **Why it exists.** Cleaning, laundry, kitchen and bathroom essentials, batteries, light bulbs, foil and wraps — the invisible provisions whose *absence* is what a household notices.
- **What belongs.** Washing-up liquid and dishwasher tablets, cleaning sprays, laundry detergent, kitchen and toilet roll, foil, cling film and bags, bin liners, batteries, bulbs.
- **How the family uses it.** Reached for by necessity, replenished by routine; the run-out that ruins a morning starts here.
- **How it reduces effort.** A kept household store is the difference between a smooth week and a string of small emergencies.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** A clean, stocked, functioning kitchen is the ground on which good cooking happens; the working house supports the eating house.
- **How it feels welcoming.** Quiet competence — the wing you never think about because it is always there, which is its own kind of care.
- **Product forms.** **Bottles, boxes, packets and rolls** — read by presence and *have / running low*, never by count.

**E2 · The pet corner**
- **Why it exists.** For the households with animals, pets are family, and their food and care are a real, recurring provision the larder must keep.
- **What belongs.** Pet food (dry and tinned), treats, litter, the animals' own supplies.
- **How the family uses it.** Fed daily, on the same rhythm as the family's own meals; the run-out is felt keenly and quickly.
- **How it reduces effort.** Kept in its own corner, the pet's provisions are never confused with the family's and never forgotten.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Care extended to the whole household, animals included; steadiness for the creatures who depend on it.
- **How it feels welcoming.** A home that keeps a corner for its animals is a home that looks after everyone in it. *(This wing is present for the households it belongs to, and quietly absent — as air and light — for those without pets; the room never insists on a shelf a household has no use for. See the honest-empty principle, § I.8.)*
- **Product forms.** **Bags, tins and boxes** — read by presence and fullness.

### Wing F — The Seasonal & Growing Room

The wing that lets the room mature. It holds what comes and goes with the year, and it reserves space for what the family will keep in future — so the room can grow for a decade without being redesigned (§ I.9).

**F1 · Seasonal storage**
- **Why it exists.** A family's keeping changes with the year — mince pies and clementines in December, jams and cordials in high summer, the things stored for a season and not the whole year.
- **What belongs.** The season's own provisions: the household's real seasonal staples, kept where they are reached for while the season lasts.
- **How the family uses it.** Filled and emptied with the calendar; a wing that is fuller at some times of year than others, and honestly emptier between.
- **How it reduces effort.** Seasonal things kept together — and only while they are in season — keep the year's rhythms out of the everyday shelves.
- **How it encourages healthier habits.** Eating with the season is eating fresher, cheaper and more varied; the room supports the household's own seasonal choices. **The room reflects the season only as the household's own provisions and never as weather or decoration** — the orchard beyond the window keeps its one season and one morning absolutely (`LIVINGHOME1`; `LIVINGHOME2` — *the passage of the year enters as the household's own things, never as the house changing*, cited).
- **How it feels welcoming.** The gentle turning of the year, felt in the larder as the family's own life — not as a theme applied to the room.
- **Product forms.** Whatever the season keeps — **jars, boxes, baskets, bottles** — always the household's real provisions, never dressing (§ II.12).

**F2 · Reserved bays — room to grow**
- **Why it exists.** A great larder is designed with **room left in it.** The reserved bays are deliberately unfilled runs of shelving that let new keeping-areas appear — preserving, fermentation, a drinks cellar, an expanded baking store — *without moving a single existing shelf.*
- **What belongs.** Nothing, yet. That is the point: **composed emptiness, never bare emptiness** (Kept Room Translation § 4.3 *Quiet corners*, cited) — space that is warm and intended, waiting to receive what the family will one day keep.
- **How it grows.** § I.9 sets out how new wings mature the room over years. The reserved bays are where they land.

## I.6 Storage philosophy — the method beneath the zones

The wings were not invented; they were *derived,* by asking of every possible area the same seven questions the brief asks. The philosophy is the method, and it is worth stating plainly so that any future area is designed the same way:

1. **Why does this exist?** — An area earns its place only if it answers a real rhythm of family life. If it does not, it is not an area; it is clutter with a label.
2. **What naturally belongs here?** — Not what a taxonomy would file here, but what a family would *reach for here.* Like lives with like because it is *used* like, not because it is *classified* like.
3. **How does it make family life easier?** — Every area must remove a decision, a search, or a worry. The measure of the room is how much the family no longer has to carry (`GEA` outcome — *less to carry*; the One Question, `THA_BRAND_CONSTITUTION.md`, cited).
4. **How does it reduce effort?** — The most-reached things at the most-reached places; the whole collection read at a glance; no counting where a look will do.
5. **How does it encourage healthier habits?** — By *placement, not instruction.* The wholefood at eye level, the fruit in the light, the good oil at hand — the room makes the healthy choice the easy one and **never grades the household for making it** (`GEA13`, cited).
6. **How does it feel welcoming?** — Warmth, order, honesty, and the readiness to receive a guest. A larder should feel like a home that is kept, not an inventory that is owed.
7. **How does it stay honest?** — An area shows only what is truly there. It never invents fullness, never implies a precision it does not have, and is warm when it is empty (§ I.8).

This philosophy is what makes the room *addable-to* without being *redesignable.* Any new area a decade from now is designed by these same seven questions and lands in a reserved bay — the room matures; it is never rebuilt.

## I.7 Product philosophy — how real products inhabit the room

The room is not designed around icons, cards or lists. It is designed around **the physical forms provisions actually take,** because *the form is how a household reads its own larder.* A jar tells its level; a tin tells its presence; a basket tells its whole contents at once. The form is information, and putting each form where it belongs is most of the room's design.

| Form | What it is | Where it belongs, and why |
|---|---|---|
| **Jar / large jar** | The decanted, level-reading staple | The Dry Store (A1–A7), the baking area, the tea/coffee caddy — *anywhere availability is best read by fullness.* The room's signature form. |
| **Tin** | The stacked, presence-reading reserve | The tinned shelf (A3), the pet corner, the hospitality store — *where the question is "do we have it in?", not "how much?"* |
| **Bottle** | The standing, pour-by-eye liquid | Oils and vinegars (A6), drinks (D2), the fridge door, cleaning sprays (E1) — *upright, near where it is used, read by level.* |
| **Packet** | The soft, flexible, back-of-shelf staple | Dry goods, baking, breakfast, frozen — *kept behind the jars, read by presence.* |
| **Box** | The stackable dry or frozen good | Cereals, crackers, tea, frozen produce, household — *the honest rectangle, read by presence.* |
| **Basket** | The open holder of things that breathe | The cool larder (C1) — *loose produce, seen whole, chosen by hand.* |
| **Loose produce** | Unpackaged fruit and vegetables | The produce baskets and the fridge drawer — *the freshest, most alive things, kept in the light so they are eaten before they turn.* |
| **Refrigerated** | Anything kept cold | The fridge (C2), by compartment — *cold is a place, and the form follows the shelf.* |
| **Frozen** | Anything kept in long cold | The freezer (C3), by drawer — *the deep reserve, read by presence.* |

The governing rule of the product philosophy: **the form is read, never counted.** A fuller jar and a lower jar; a stocked shelf and a sparse one; a full basket and a nearly-empty one — these are the room's whole language of availability. It never resolves to a number, because a household does not know its own larder as numbers, and a number the room does not truly own would be a claim it cannot keep (`LARDER1` §§ 3, 14, 16 — *never a quantity*; Core Principle 6, cited).

## I.8 Reading the room — availability by looking

A household reads its larder the way it reads a familiar face: at a glance, and mostly by what has changed. The room's availability language has exactly three honest states, and each is carried by **more than colour** (`LARDER1` § 10, cited — read without relying on colour or fullness alone):

- **Kept and stocked** — the jar full, the shelf populated, the basket generous. Shown by a fuller vessel and a settled place. *The household has this; nothing to do.*
- **Running low** — the jar down to its last, the shelf sparse. Shown by a low vessel *and* a plain, un-shaming cue (a word, a mark) — *never guilt, never a warning tone* (Kept Room Translation § 4.2 *Shelving* — "freshness is helpful words, never guilt"; cited). *The household may want to add this to the shop — offered, never nagged.*
- **Out, or no longer kept** — a gap on the shelf where the thing lived. An *out* item is a gap that can be filled from the shop; a *no-longer-kept* item is a gap the household chose (the explicit "take it out of the larder" gesture, `LARDER1` §§ 4.2, 5, cited). The room never hides that something is gone, and never pretends a shelf holds more than it does (Kept Room Translation § 4.2 *Shelving* — "no fake fullness… the plain truth of what is there"; cited).

Three disciplines hold this language honest:

- **No invented fullness.** The room shows the availability its owner truly holds (the household's *have* signal and their own sense) and nothing finer. A low-looking jar is *a feeling, not a measurement* (`LARDER1` § 14, cited).
- **No time it does not own.** No expiry, best-before, purchase date or shelf life — a low jar is *approximate availability, not a countdown* (`LARDER1` § 3; Domain 30 owns no time; cited). The room may help a household *see* what is fresh by keeping fresh things in view (C1–C3); it never *tracks* a date.
- **The empty shelf is a designed state, and it is warm.** A bare area is **composed emptiness, never bare emptiness** — light and quiet air, an open invitation to keep something, never a cold or accusing blank (Kept Room Translation § 4.3 *Quiet corners*; Experience Language § 3A.4 — *calm must never become lifeless*; cited). A household with no pets simply sees no pet corner; a family that has not started preserving simply sees a warm reserved bay. **Honest absence is hospitable here, never a gap to be filled by fabrication** (Core Principle 6, cited).

## I.9 Growth — how the room matures over the years, without redesign

A family home's larder is never finished. It deepens: a household starts baking more and the baking area grows; someone takes up preserving and a preserving shelf appears; the drinks a home keeps for guests earn a wing of their own. **The Living Larder is designed so this maturing never requires a redesign** — because the furniture came first (§ I.4) and the reserved bays (F2) were left in hand for exactly this.

**The Growth Law.** *The room's identity is permanent; new areas appear within it, never in place of it.* A new area is admitted only when it passes the same seven questions (§ I.6) every existing wing passed — it must answer a real rhythm of family life, not a wish to add a feature. When it does, it lands in a reserved bay and the room matures by one shelf, its light, materials, and character untouched.

Natural growth the room is already shaped to receive:

- **Preserving.** As a household makes its own jams, chutneys and cordials, the preserves shelf (A7) deepens into a preserving area — the jars the family filled themselves, kept and read exactly as the bought ones are.
- **Fermentation.** Sourdough starters, kimchi, kefir, kombucha — a small, living, cool corner near the cool store. A genuinely new rhythm (things that are *tended,* not just kept), earning a bay of its own.
- **A drinks store / cellar.** As the hospitality drinks (D2) outgrow their shelf, they become a wing — kept bottles for guests and occasions, read by presence.
- **Baking expansion.** The occasional baking area (A4) grows into a baker's shelf — more flours, more sugars, the equipment kept close.
- **Entertaining.** Beyond the hospitality store (D1), a home that entertains often keeps serving things, table things, the provisions of a gathering — a wing the room can grow toward.
- **Seasonal collections.** The seasonal wing (F1) deepens as a household's own traditions settle (`LIVINGHOME1` Traditions domain, cited as the owner of *what a household declares it keeps* — the Larder never assumes an occasion; it reflects only what the family has said is theirs).

The room should **mature like a real family home** — slowly, by the family's own life, each new area earned and warm. It should never be *versioned,* re-skinned, or reorganised out from under the household (Kept Room Translation § 4.3 *Permanence* — "no trend, no re-skin, no fashionable redesign"; cited). A family that has kept this larder for ten years should find it deeper, fuller, and more theirs — and still, unmistakably, the same room.

# PART II — THE BINDING

*How the room of Part I stands on THA's existing owners, minting not one new owner of any fact. This part is where the interior architecture meets the governing canon; on any conflict, the owners below prevail (`LARDER1` scope; `CRAFT1` § 1).*

## II.10 The Two-Layer Law — the Permanent Room and the Living Contents

The whole binding rests on one distinction, which is also the mission's own instruction (*the room is permanent; the products simply live within it*):

> **Layer 1 — The Permanent Room (presentation) owns no fact.** The wings, the shelving, the spice rack, the baking counter, the reserved bays, the fullness language — the entire architecture of Part I — is a *presentation.* It exists whether or not any product populates it, and it stores nothing. An empty spice rack is still a spice rack. This layer is authored here and by the room's implementation; it is a *reading* of the household's provisions, never a record of them.
>
> **Layer 2 — The Living Contents are read from the owners, and written only to them.** Every actual jar, tin, bottle and basket in the room is a fact owned elsewhere and untouched by this document:
>
> | The fact | Its one owner (unchanged, cited) |
> |---|---|
> | *What the household keeps · which storage place it lives in · its approximate availability* | **Domain 30 — Pantry State.** `user_pantry_items` (`shared/schema.ts`); `server/storage.ts` the only writer (`LARDER1` § 2, cited). |
> | *What a food **is** — its identity, family, and knowledge* | **Domain 2 — Canonical Food Identity.** Resolved by `ingredientKey` (`LARDER1` § 2, cited). |
> | *What needs buying* | **Domain 15 — Shopping State.** The room *originates* an entry; it holds no rival list (`LARDER1` § 8, cited). |
> | *The season* | **Domain 11**, consumed where already exposed; never re-owned (`LARDER1` § 2, cited). |
> | *Companion enrichment* | The registered, permission-aware `pantry` capability; owns no room data (`LARDER1` § 9, cited). |

**This is why a richer room than the six stored categories is lawful.** `LARDER1` § 3 requires the room to *"read the column that exists"* and forbids owning a *second categorisation* (Principle 2). The Two-Layer Law honours that exactly: the room's many *presented* areas are not a second stored taxonomy — they are **one presentation, reading facts two owners already hold.** A jar of cumin is `category: "larder"` in Domain 30 (a storage *place*) and *a spice* in Domain 2 (a food *identity*); presenting it on the spice rack within the larder wing invents no stored fact — it reads the two the owners already keep. The room owns the *arrangement;* it never owns the *fact.*

## II.12 What the room must never become

This interior architecture is bound by every non-goal `LARDER1` § 16 already fixed, and adds the ones its physical richness makes newly tempting:

- **Never a quantities or measures feature.** No amounts, units, or counts anywhere. Approximate availability only (`LARDER1` § 16, cited). A fuller jar is never "1.4 kg."
- **Never a freshness, expiry, or ageing tracker.** Domain 30 owns no time; the physical metaphor must not smuggle one in (`LARDER1` §§ 3, 16, cited). A low jar is not a countdown.
- **Never a second owner of buying or of food identity.** Shopping (Domain 15) remains the sole owner of what needs buying; Canonical Food (Domain 2) the sole owner of what a food is (`LARDER1` §§ 2, 8, 16, cited). Marking an item needed writes only Domain 15 and never removes the staple — the load-bearing rule (`LARDER1` § 8, cited).
- **Never a second stored categorisation.** The presented areas are a reading, not a taxonomy; the room stores only the six enforced places, and grows them only by a governed act (§ II.11, cited).
- **Never dressing, and never a claim.** This is the newly-sharp one. The room's baskets of fruit, jars, and seasonal shelves are **the household's own provisions — data-borne** — and never decorative props. **No produce dressing in the Pantry room** (`LIVINGHOME2` § 5.1 / `ED3`, cited): a bowl of apples the room *invented* for warmth is refused here; a basket of the family's *real* onions is the room doing its job. Dressing carries zero information (`ED4`) and reads no household data (`ED2`); the Larder's contents are nothing but information the household owns. The line is absolute: **if the room put it there, it is refused; if the household keeps it, it belongs.**
- **Never a place that grades the household.** No score, rank, streak, target, or reward; no *"you let the flour run out."* The room shows the food; it never grades the family (`GEA13`; `LARDER1` § 16, cited). The healthy default is offered by *placement,* never by judgement (§ I.6, point 5).
- **Never a room only a pointer can operate.** Drag is an enhancement; every outcome is reachable without it, keyboard- and screen-reader-operable, announced as the physical thing it is (`LARDER1` § 10, cited).
- **Never a theme, a re-skin, or a costume.** No painted jars, no drawn produce, no faux timber, no second sun — the room is warm and real *because it is true,* never dressed up to seem so (Kept Room Translation § 4.2 *Shelving*/*Oak*, cited through to Blueprint § 16 *the theme park*; `CRAFT1` § 5 — *beautifully real, never fantasy*, cited).

## II.13 Craftsmanship and feeling

The room is held to the Craftsmanship Standard (`CRAFT1` § 1, cited) and must produce the feelings the canon requires, without ever counterfeiting them:

- **The seven feelings** (Experience Language § 3, cited): calm · welcoming · effortless · intelligent · reassuring · premium · quietly memorable — *without becoming theatrical, gimmicky, or distracting.* The Larder's premium is the perceptible result of care taken on the household's behalf — the whole room warm, kept, and honest — never ornament or expense.
- **The emotional palette** (Experience Language § 3A, cited): the room must feel *calm · warm · energised · thoughtful · comforting · decisive · curious,* and must **never** feel *cold · clinical · empty · silent · sterile · funeral-parlour calm.* Its governing note applies with full force to a room full of empty-shelf states: **calm must never become lifeless** (§ 3A.4, cited).
- **Beautifully real** (`CRAFT1` § 5, cited): never fantasy, never gamified, never decorative for its own sake. Every jar shown is a jar the household keeps; every fullness is a fullness they have; every empty shelf is honestly empty and warm.
- **The quality standard** (`CRAFT1` § 8, cited): the room is finished only when the Home Owner can answer the one question — **"Would I happily spend time here?"** — with *yes.* A larder a family would happily stand in, on an ordinary evening, deciding what to cook, is the whole target.

Final aesthetic approval of the room's character — its warmth, materials, light, and the admission of any future area — rests with the **Home Owner** (`HOMEOWNER1`, cited); this document supplies the interior architecture the Home Owner approves against, and owns no aesthetic value the UI Architecture, the Experience Language, or the Kept Room Translation already own.
